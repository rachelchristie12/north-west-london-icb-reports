Executive Summary

North West London (NWL) has a young and relatively healthy population, but significant inequalities exist across ethnicity, gender, age, and deprivation. This report aims to understand the variation in need and unmet need in NWL, in order to highlight priority areas for NWL and influence planning.

Key Insights
  • Children: Gaps in vaccination, obesity, dental health, and mental health support.
  • Adults: Challenges with cancer screening, obesity, sexual health, severe mental health, depression, and anxiety.
  • Older Adults: High service demand from hypertension, diabetes, obesity, stroke mortality, and falls.
Cross-Cutting Priorities
  • Mental Health:
    • 30% higher rates of Mental Health Act detentions than England, especially among Black communities, men, and 18–34s.
    • Severe Mental Illness (SMI) patients rely heavily on A&E and acute care, highlighting poor access to early support.
    • Stigma and low trust reduce service use, particularly for older adults.
  • Cardio-Renal Metabolic Conditions:
    • Hypertension, diabetes, and obesity drive major health burdens.
    • Obesity is increasing among both children and adults, with higher rates in deprived, Black, and Asian groups.
    • These conditions fuel inequalities and worsen long-term outcomes.
Inequalities in Care
  • Health outcomes and service use vary sharply by deprivation and ethnicity, with higher emergency use, alcohol misuse, and diabetes in these groups.
  • Patients report barriers such as poor communication, lack of cultural sensitivity, and low trust in NHS services.
  • Spending is highest in deprived, reflecting later, more expensive interventions.
Implications for NWL ICB
  • Prioritise mental health, obesity, hypertension, and diabetes.
  • Embed equity and cultural competence into service design.
  • Improve access, communication, and trust, especially for deprived and minority communities.
  • Shift from reactive care to prevention, especially for healthy populations.

Who are the people living in North West London

The people registered in the North West London Integrated Care Board (NWL ICB) area represent a wide range of cultural, ethnic, and socio-economic backgrounds. The area includes eight boroughs Brent, Ealing, Harrow, Hillingdon, Hammersmith and Fulham, Kensington and Chelsea, Westminster, and Hounslow—each with its own unique communities.

Breaking the population into smaller groups helps the ICB better understand and address health needs by recognizing that different groups experience varying levels of risk, access and outcomes. By dividing the population based on factors such as age, socioeconomic status, ethnicity, geography, or specific health conditions, we can identify patterns in disease prevalence, access to care, and service usage. This enables more targeted interventions, efficient resource allocation, and tailored strategies, ultimately improving health equity and ensuring that services meet the needs of communities rather than applying a one-size-fits-all approach.

In this report we look at the population by gender, deprivation, ethnicity and whole population segments. The NWL whole population segments comprise of 13 segments that captures distinct health requirements. They are designed to ensure that the groups were both familiar and pragmatic so that they would be easy to use in the actual context of North West London and to make it easy for professionals to work together.

The majority (>50%) of the NWL population is made up of healthy adults under 65, followed by adults (18-65) with 1 or more long term conditions. Despite the other population groups making up a smaller proportion of the population our more complex groups have a big impact on services as we will see below.

Overall, NWL has a young population compared to the national average, especially in central boroughs, where there are high numbers of working-age adults and young professionals. Boroughs such as Harrow, Hillingdon, and Ealing have growing older populations, with a notable proportion of residents aged 65 and over. At the same time, many NWL boroughs also have a high proportion of children and young people, particularly in Brent and Hounslow, reflecting family-oriented communities.

Ealing residents make up the largest share of the North West London ICB population, followed by Brent. A smaller proportion of registered patients live outside the eight NWL boroughs, most commonly in neighboring areas such as Barnet, Camden, Richmond upon Thames, and Wandsworth.

Many residents are from Black and Asian ethnic backgrounds, and a significant proportion speak languages other than English at home, most commonly Arabic, Punjabi and Gujarati. The population includes both affluent neighborhoods and areas facing high levels of deprivation, resulting in varied health and social care needs. This diversity presents both challenges and opportunities for delivering equitable, inclusive, and culturally sensitive healthcare across the region.

GP records indicate that English is the main language for only 36% of the population, with Arabic, Punjabi, and Gujarati being the most common languages after English. However, around a third of the GP language data is missing from the GP record and the ONS reports that between 69-79% of people have their main language as English in NWL. This still much lower than the national average of 91%.

Health Status in North West London

Life expectancy in North West London is still recovering from the impact of COVID-19, while healthy life expectancy has remained relatively stable. There is significant variation across the region. Communities experiencing higher levels of deprivation tend to have both lower life expectancy and lower healthy life expectancy. As a result, people in these areas are likely to spend a greater proportion of their lives in poor health. However, the gap in overall life expectancy hasn’t always fallen to the same extent as healthy life expectancy. That means they often spend more years in poor health compared with those in more affluent areas ONS, 2022.

Impact on the health service

The graph below illustrates how both age and patient needs influence levels of unplanned healthcare activity. As individuals develop long-term conditions (LTCs), their likelihood of requiring urgent or emergency care increases, placing greater demand on NHS services. Keeping the population healthier for longer — particularly by preventing or delaying the onset of long-term conditions — can significantly reduce the overall need for unplanned care. Moreover, slowing the progression of individuals from lower-need groups (those with no or a single LTC) into more complex groups, such as those living with dementia or severe mental illness, can ease pressure on the system. This idea aligns with a population health management perspective, focusing on prevention, early intervention, and tailored care to improve outcomes while ensuring the sustainability of resources.

What influences a persons health

Health outcomes are shaped by a complex interaction of factors, with social and economic circumstances exerting the greatest influence (around 40%), followed by health behaviors such as diet, physical activity, and smoking (30%). Health services account for approximately 20% of overall health impact, while the physical environment contributes around 10%. This distribution aligns with the evidence on the wider determinants of health, as described in the Marmot Review and subsequent analyses by The King’s Fund. Recognising these proportions highlights the need for a population health approach that goes beyond clinical care to address the social, economic, and behavioral factors that drive health inequalities and influence long‑term outcomes.

Social, Economic and Environment (Wider determinants of health)

Wider determinants of health—such as income, education, housing, employment, environment, and social connections—play a major role in shaping life expectancy. While access to healthcare contributes to overall health, research shows that these broader social, economic, and environmental factors have a far greater impact on how long and how well people live. For instance, individuals living in deprived areas often face insecure employment, poor-quality housing, and limited access to healthy food or safe environments, all of which increase the risk of chronic illness and reduce life expectancy. Addressing and understanding these wider determinants is therefore essential to narrowing health inequalities and improving population health outcomes.

Unemployment rate is defined as the proportion of the economically active population (aged 16+) who are without a job.

Unemployment is a significant issue in North West London. All boroughs, apart from H&F have higher unemployment rates than the England average. Hillingdon has the highest unemployment rate out of the NWL boroughs, whilst Kensington and Chelsea has the lowest employment rate (62.6%) and highest economic inactivity rate (other) (25.3%) nationally.

In NWL the leading drivers of unemployment due to sickness are Mental and behavioral disorders and MSK, futhermore, economic activity is increasing in the younger age groups (18-34) and decreasing in the 35-49 group.

Link between work and health
Unemployment or poor-quality work harms health; unemployment is associated with higher levels of stress, depression, anxiety, and poorer physical health outcomes (e.g., increased cardiovascular risk). Insecure or poor-quality work can also harm health through low pay, high stress, and unsafe conditions. (Bambra, 2019)

Health affects ability to work; chronic illness, disability, and poor mental health often reduce people’s ability to enter or remain in work. This can create a negative cycle. (DWP). In NWL, 3.5% of people are economically inactive due to long-term sickness or disability, however this is less that then England average (4.1%).

Early intervention helps break the cycle; Integrated approaches—such as social prescribing, vocational rehabilitation, and employment support embedded in health services—help people stay in or return to work, improving long-term outcomes.(DWP)

Low pay rate is defined as the proportion of employees earning below the real Living Wage as set by the Living Wage Foundation. In 2023–24, the real Living Wage was set at £13.15 in London and £12.00 in the rest of the UK.

Despite boroughs in London generally being higher earners than other parts of England, there is still an issue with low pay. In Harrow, nearly 30% of all employees earn below minimum wage, this is the fourth highest low pay rate nationally.

In NWL, around 30% of all children live in poverty, with Brent and Hounslow reporting higher child poverty rates than the national average, equally, in Westminster 40.3% of pupils receive free school meals.

Link between income and health
Financial stress & basic needs – Low pay limits access to essentials like nutritious food, safe housing, and heating, which are key to maintaining good health. This financial strain also contributes to chronic stress, which is linked to poor mental and physical health.

Work insecurity & poor conditions – Low-paid jobs are often insecure, involve shift work or unsafe environments, and provide little control or autonomy, all of which negatively affect wellbeing.

Mental health impact – People in low-paid roles report higher rates of anxiety, depression, and poorer overall life satisfaction compared to those on adequate incomes.(Marmot,2020)

Early life disadvantage – Children growing up in poverty are more likely to experience poor nutrition, inadequate housing, and limited access to safe spaces for play, which directly affect physical development and long‑term health.

Stress & mental health – Living in poverty exposes children to chronic stress, family financial strain, and, in some cases, adverse childhood experiences, all of which are linked to higher rates of anxiety, depression, and behavioural issues.

Long‑term health inequalities – Children in the most deprived areas are more likely to develop chronic illnesses earlier, experience worse health in adulthood, and have shorter life expectancy than their peers from wealthier families. (Marmot,2020) (PHE,2014).

Non-decent homes are defined as those with a Category 1 hazard – as assessed by the Housing Health and Safety Rating System (HHSRS) – that are not in a reasonable state of repair, lack reasonably modern facilities or are not warm enough (do not provide a reasonable degree of thermal comfort).

Six out of the eight boroughs are above the national average for non-decent socially rented homes and in small areas up to 39% of housing is overcrowded ONS census data. Furthermore, Westminster and Ealing boroughs have higher rates of people in temporary accommodation compared to the London average (17.2 householder per 1,000 population), these living conditions are associated in increased healthcare need. Link between housing and health
Quality of housing – Poor‑quality homes (damp, cold, overcrowded, or unsafe) are linked to higher rates of respiratory illness, infections, injuries, and poor mental health.

Affordability & insecurity – High housing costs and insecure tenancies increase stress, financial strain, and instability, which are associated with anxiety, depression, and poorer overall wellbeing.

Overcrowding – Living in overcrowded conditions raises the risk of infectious diseases, disrupts sleep, and negatively impacts children’s educational attainment and emotional development.

Location & environment – Housing in areas with poor access to green space, healthy food, transport, and healthcare can compound health inequalities.

Homelessness & temporary accommodation – People experiencing homelessness or living in temporary housing face extremely poor health outcomes, with much lower life expectancy and higher rates of chronic illness. (Marmot,2020)

Active travel is defined as Walking, cycling, and public transport use encourage physical activity, reducing the risk of obesity, cardiovascular disease, and diabetes. In North West London, the inner boroughs tend to have a greater percentage of active travel compared to the outer boroughs.

Link between transport and health
Access to services & opportunities – Good transport links improve access to healthcare, education, employment, and social connections, which are vital for physical and mental wellbeing.

Air quality & road safety – High traffic volumes contribute to air pollution (linked to respiratory and cardiovascular disease) and increase the risk of road traffic injuries.

Cost & inequality – Limited or expensive transport disproportionately affects low‑income groups, restricting access to jobs, healthy food, and leisure activities, which worsens health inequalities.

Isolation & mental health – Poor transport connectivity increases social isolation, particularly for older adults and people with disabilities, contributing to loneliness and poorer mental health. (Marmot,2020).

In North West London, there are is generally a lower percentage of people with no qualifications the national average, however the trend differs for pupils missing school, suggesting a potential shift in educational attainment in the future. In Westminster over a quarter of the pupils miss 10% of the school classes. Across NWL, males, children with SEN support, children whose first language is English and children with free school meals (FSM) have higher rates of persistent absentees, with 25% children who are FSM eligible being persistent absentees.

Persistent absenteeism by ethnicity, varies across London boroughs, but generally absenteeism is higher in the White and Mixed ethnic groups compared to Asian and Black UK Gov, 2024/25.

Link between education and health Education and life chances – Higher educational attainment is strongly linked to better employment opportunities, higher income, and more stable housing — all of which support better health outcomes.

Health literacy – Education improves people’s ability to understand health information, navigate services, and make healthier lifestyle choices.

Mental wellbeing – Achieving qualifications boosts self‑esteem and life satisfaction, while low attainment is linked to poorer mental health and increased risk of unemployment.

Intergenerational effects – Parents’ education levels influence children’s health and development, perpetuating cycles of advantage or disadvantage

Missed learning & attainment – High levels of school absence are associated with lower educational achievement, which impacts long‑term health outcomes and employment opportunities.

Safeguarding & wellbeing – Persistent absence can be linked to safeguarding issues, family stress, or health problems, making it a marker for wider vulnerabilities.

Social development – Time away from school reduces opportunities for social interaction and support networks, affecting children’s emotional wellbeing and resilience.

Cycle of disadvantage – Children with frequent absences are more likely to leave education with fewer qualifications, perpetuating poorer health outcomes into adulthood.

Loneliness is the proportion of people who respond to the question ‘How often do you feel lonely?’ with ‘Often’ or ‘Always’ in hr Community Life Survey 2023/24.

In North West London, three of the boroughs have a higher proportion of people who often feel lonely, with Brent having the highest proportion out of all boroughs. Westminster has the lowest proportion of people who chat to their neighbors at least once per month nationally with only 51.5%

Link between community and health Social support and belonging – Strong community ties and cultural connections provide emotional support, reduce feelings of isolation, and promote mental wellbeing.

Identity and resilience – Cultural identity helps build self-esteem and resilience, contributing to positive mental health outcomes.

Health behaviours – Cultural norms influence health behaviours such as diet, exercise, and attitudes toward healthcare, shaping overall health outcomes.

Access to resources – Inclusive communities often facilitate better access to health information, services, and social activities that promote wellbeing.

Reducing inequalities – Supporting cultural diversity and community engagement helps address social exclusion and reduce health disparities.

Deprivation

The severity of many of the wider determinants of health—such as income, employment, education and housing — is effectively summarized by the Index of Multiple Deprivation (IMD). The IMD brings together data across several domains to create a single measure of relative deprivation, highlighting how disadvantage in one area often overlaps with others. This makes the IMD a crucial tool for understanding and addressing the root causes of health disparities.

The map below shows which geographic areas are in each deprivation decile (1-10), with 1 being the most deprived and 10 being the least deprived. This report also analyses the data by deprivation quintiles (1-5), with 1 representing the most deprived (deciles 1-2) or the Core20 group.

Demographics of the Core 20: When comparing patients living in the least deprived (IMD 10) and most deprived (IMD 1) areas of the NWL, those living in the least deprived areas are younger than those in most deprived areas. Analysis of ethnicity shows a much higher percentage of black patients in most deprived areas, and a lower percentage of Asian and White ethnicity.


Health Behaviours

Health behaviors play a crucial role in shaping health outcomes; by making conscious, positive choices in daily life, people can significantly reduce health risks, manage conditions more effectively, and improve both their lifespan and quality of life. This report looks into negative health behaviors such as, smoking, alcohol misuse and physical inactivity and positive health behaviors, cancer screening, blood pressure checks, STI testing and vaccination.

Overall, North West London has high rates in detrimental health behaviors compared to England with the exception of smoking. North West London performs the most poorly in admissions for alcohol specific conditions, STI test positivity and bowel screening. There is established trend between deprivation and detrimental health behaviors, from the data available the largest inequality is seen in alcohol admissions followed by smoking and physical inactivity. Based off these data point address alcohol misuse is a key issue in North West London.

Smoking is a major health behavior that strongly affects health outcomes. It damages the lungs and blood vessels, greatly increasing the risk of diseases such as lung cancer, heart disease, and stroke. Quitting smoking, on the other hand, quickly improves health by lowering these risks and enhancing overall well-being.

Smoking prevalence has been decreasing the NWL over the last five years, however it is an area with a high degree of inequity. Groups with higher rates of smoking that could be reviewed for further targeting are those in the most deprived areas, Black and Black British, males, and those with severe mental health, particularly people with SMI under 65.

Patient Insights
  • Smoking rates are very high among people with SMI, yet it seems to be rarely addressed in mental health treatment plans.
  • Children in complex families are frequently exposed to smoking at home, with suggested little evidence of targeted public health messaging.
  • People with LTCs face high relapse risks, often linked to pain, stress, or lack of ongoing cessation support.
  • Some in the healthy population feel stigmatised about smoking and avoid seeking help to quit.
  • People with LD may not always be aware of the health risks of smoking or where to get support.

Regular physical activity is a key health behavior that greatly improves health outcomes. It helps prevent chronic diseases such as heart disease, diabetes, and obesity, while also boosting mental well-being and quality of life. In contrast, a lack of physical activity increases the risk of poor health and early mortality.

Physical inactivity as remained stable over the last five years, however it is an area with a high degree of inequity. Groups with higher rates of physical inactivity that could be reviewed for further targeting are those in the most deprived areas, Asian and Asian British, females, and those with learning disability, particularly those over 65.

Practicing safe sexual health behaviors, such as using protection and getting regular screenings, reduces the risk of sexually transmitted infections and unplanned pregnancies. Positive sexual health practices also support overall well-being, healthy relationships, and reproductive health.

In the last NWL STI test positivity has increase above London, this suggests NWL is not performing enough tests compared the need created by the true prevalence of sexual health disease.

Alcohol admissions have increased over the last three years in NWL, a trend that is not seen nationally or in London. Alcohol admissions report the highest degree of inequity in deprivation, gender and ethnicity. Those who are most affected are the those in the most deprived group, White ethnic group, males and people with severe mental health, particulary those over 65.

Patient Insights
  • Some people with LTCs use alcohol to cope with pain, isolation, or emotional distress.
  • In people with early-stage dementia, alcohol misuse may go unnoticed or be attributed to other symptoms.
  • Some people with SMI use alcohol to self-manage symptoms or emotional discomfort.
  • Concerns have been raised about parental alcohol use in families with children who have complex needs.
  • Alcohol misuse in the healthy population is rarely acknowledged or discussed openly.
  • Among people with LD, alcohol use may go undetected due to difficulties in communication and lack of routine screening.

Vaccination is a critical health behavior that protects individuals and communities from infectious diseases. Staying up to date with recommended vaccines lowers the risk of severe illness, prevents outbreaks, and promotes long-term public health. The needs assessments focus on two prominent vaccinations, MMR and Influenza.

MMR is given to children under 5 which protects against measles, mumps and rubella. There has been a constant decline in childhood vaccines over the last decade UKHSA, 2023 and both London and NWL are below the national average. However as of 2022/23, North West London is performing better than the London average. Data for North West London inequalities is unavailble for the report, however nationally MMR vaccine uptake is lower in the most deprived regions OHID, 2022/23. Furthermore, children born to mothers in minority ethnic groups had lower vaccination coverage and inequities have been seen to widen over the between 2006 and 2021 particularly in Caribbean, Other Black and African and Caribbean groups, Lancet, 2023

Influenza vaccinations are primarily given to those aged 65+ to reduce the transmission and severity of flu, the predominant winter virus in the UK. As of 2023/24, NWL has the worst vaccination coverage for flu in England at 64.4% OHID, 2023/24. Those in more deprived area, females, Black or Black British are all groups that could be targeted to improved uptake. Equally, those in the Healthy 65+ group have much lower coverage compared to the rest of the 65+ group.

NB: WSIC data underestimates flu vaccine coverage compared to OHID source

Cancer screening and blood pressure (BP) checks are important because they help detect problems early, often before symptoms appear. Early detection of cancer or high blood pressure can lead to quicker treatment, better outcomes, and reduced risk of serious complications. Regular checks also give people peace of mind and encourage healthier lifestyle choices.

Bowel screening has increased over the last five years, however North West London remains far below the National average and below comparable ICB, Greater Manchester. NWL has the worst uptake of screening for both bowel and cervical cancer.

NWL had a lower rate on urgent suspected cancer referrals to the national average, which may be reflective of the poor screening uptake. However, there is also a low percentage of the urgent referrals that result in cancer diagnosis suggesting, that either the referrals are targeting the wrong people and these people are being diagnosed elsewhere or the rate of referrals is suitable for the true prevalence of cancer in NWL OHID, 2024. Futhermore, data from the National Disease Registration Service suggests that NWL has a higher incidence of diagnosis through A&E, meaning that people may not be accessing the right services.

Patient Insights
  • Some healthy individuals do not engage with preventative care, due to other commitments
  • It appears that some healthy individuals have undiagnosed high blood pressure, particularly where community health check uptake is low.
  • People with dementia and LD often miss cancer screening invitations or struggle to understand their purpose.
  • People with SMI may avoid cancer screening due to anxiety, mistrust, or negative past experiences.
  • Some in the healthy population delay seeking help due to unawareness of early cancer symptoms.
  • Among people with LTCs, cancer is sometimes discovered incidentally during routine or unrelated care

Bowel screening has the highest inequality between deprivation groups, meaning the most deprived group could be targeted for better uptake. Particularly those in the Healthy under 18-64 group, which also has a low uptake compared to the other whole population segments.

In cervical screening, the inequality is lesser between ethnic groups and deprived in compared to bowel screening. However, there is still poor uptake of cervical screening compared to other ICBs. Those in the other ethnic group and people with learning disability have the lowest uptake.

Health Service

Access to healthcare services strongly shapes health outcomes because timely, affordable, and appropriate care can prevent illnesses from worsening, support early diagnosis, and improve management of chronic conditions. When access is limited—whether due to cost, distance, long waiting times, or lack of culturally appropriate services—people are more likely to experience avoidable complications, poorer quality of life, and reduced life expectancy. Understanding activity rates can aid understanding of access to services; increased reactive emergency services usage can suggest poorer access to more preventative care. Comparing North West London (NWL) rates to national figures and to different segments within NWL helps identify pressure points within the system.

Increased activity is not necessarily a bad thing if it is meeting the need of the population, for example increased GP appointments in a a population that has higher rates of poor health. The graph below investigates the rate of activity compared to the prevalence of multi-morbidity, which can be used as proxy indicator for general healthcare need. The top graph compares this ratio between deprivation groups and the bottom compared across ethnic groups.

Across the deprivation groups those in the most deprived groups receive more service activity per multi-morbid patient for A&E, GP appointments and unplanned bed days. It is notable that despite the increase the primary care activity per multi-morbid patient that activity per patient in A&E and unplanned bed days still reports some inequality. This may reflect the exacerbated need caused by living in a deprived area resulting in more trips to A&E and longer stays in hospital. However, it is worth noting that the difference in activity per patient in primary care is greater than the reactive care, suggesting the support given in primary care may reduce potentially inequalities in activity that could be seen in secondary reactive care.

Across deprivation groups, patients in the most deprived areas receive more service activity per multi-morbid patient in A&E, GP appointments, and unplanned bed days. Notably, while activity per multi-morbid patient increases in primary care for the more deprived groups, so does A&E use and unplanned bed days. This may reflect the greater health needs associated with living in deprived areas, leading to more A&E visits and longer hospital stays, or may suggest that more than increased primary care activity is needed to reduce secondary reactive care. However, the difference in activity per patient is greater in primary care than in reactive care, suggesting that enhanced primary care support may help reduce inequalities that would otherwise appear more strongly in secondary, reactive services.

Across ethnic groups, the picture is more complex. Conclusion

In North West London, there is a young and generally healthy population compared to England. However, there are populations that are less healthy compared to national average; Asian and Black ethnicity and over 65s. Equally, we have significant variation in the health status of our population with those in the more deprived communities having a shorted healthy life span. Addressing these inequalities requires an understanding of health outcomes, unmet needs, and the risk factors that shape them.

The table shows some of the key areas for improvement across the different population segments. These topics are the risk behaviours and outcomes that are significantly worse than England, have high prevalence in the segment or have a large degree of inequality.

Understanding the different areas of unmet need in each segment, allows the ICB to focus in on the biggest issues for each population, ultimately providing a better service and improving population health outcomes. In particular understanding and addressing the needs of the Healthy population enables the ICB to start focusing on moving from sickness to prevention, one of the main themes from the NHS 10-year plan.

Cross-cutting areas of unmet need

Mental Health
North West London has increased need compared to England in a number of areas; most notably people with severe mental health conditions. NWL has nearly 30% more people being subject to the Mental Health Act per 100K population compared to England. North West London, has a similar prevalence of severe mental health (SMI) to England, therefore the substantial increase people being subject to the Mental Health Act suggests the support for these patients is lacking. The rate of Mental health detentions is higher in the Black population, Males and people aged 18-34, this highlights groups not getting the right care.

Mental health activity is higher in people with SMI, and significantly more in those with SMI aged 18-64 compared to those over 65, suggesting those over 65 may not be getting the mental health support they need or being treated in other parts of the system People with SMI currently put burden across all services with high rates of A&E attendances, outpatients and unplanned bed days. Many people with SMI are referred to Mental Health Service via A&E, rates are also higher in those aged 18-65, the Core 20 population and Black and Mixed ethnicity. Furthermore, Mental Health services are likely to see increased demand as anxiety and depression are the 2nd and 3rd LTCs with the highest absolute increase in prevalence. This trend can also be seen in the the rate of mental health contacts in the healthy children populations, which is higher than the healthy adult population. This suggests that the mental health support for children may be more accessible compared to adults but it might also suggest increased expectation and demand for these services as this population ages.

Patient insights also highlight that there is stigma around mental health which may lead to avoidance of service , ultimately leading to poorer outcomes in the long term.

Cardio Renal Metabolic Conditions
The LTCs that are have the biggest impact in NWL are Hypertension, Diabetes and Obesity, there are all conditions related to cardio renal metabolic pathway. Obesity is also considered a more upstream risk factor for diabetes and hypertension and all three LTCs are risk factors for more severe LTCs such as CHD and heart failure.

Diabetes is the only condition where they prevalence is higher that the national average for the whole population. In the over 65 group the difference between NWL and national diabetes prevalence increases and the NWL hypertension prevalence also exceeds the national average. Obesity prevalence has had the greatest absolute charges in prevalence and the 3rd highest relative change in prevalence over the last 3 years. Furthermore, the Obesity prevalence is likely underestimated by the QoF definition used in this report. Children in NWL are also affected with children in Year 6 having a higher prevalence of obesity compared to the national average. Higher rates of obesity are seen the in the most deprived populations and Black and Asian. This conditions is likely a considerable driving factor in the reduced percentage of healthy people in those populations.

There is also evidence of inequality of care by how much we are spending on different ethnic and deprivation groups; there is a higher spend in the White, Black and more deprived populations, suggesting they experience more severe and costly health care

The analysis shows inequalities across most health outcomes, especially in service use. It also highlights that there common factors that being from a deprived or ethnic background that will contribute to all health outcomes. Survey evidence reveals that a substantial proportion of patients (especially ethnic minorities) report discrimination from NHS providers, leading to reduced engagement, avoidance of care, and mistrust—all linked to poorer health outcomes. Black, Asian, and other minority groups consistently report worse experiences at multiple points in the care pathway, such as feeling dismissed, facing language barriers, or lacking culturally appropriate services. NHS RHO, 2025. Equally, those in the most deprived populations face similar issues due to lock of trust with public institutions which can result from the fact the institutions are set up in a way that make it harder for these groups to access.Kings Fund, 2024
